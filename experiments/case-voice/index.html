01 · Verdict

Short and clean, but it sounds like a summary of you, not you.

The site has already lost most of the obvious machine words: no "seamless", no "leverage", no "passionate". What's left is subtler, and a design leader reading properly will still feel it:

  • Every sentence lands. Paragraphs end on a neat line ("That was the trade…", "Neither side oversells."). People don't write like that for a whole page. Andreas lets most sentences just carry a fact and saves the punchline for one or two.
  • There's no scene. No room, no day, no person in it. Andreas opens with "three of us had three weeks"; ours opens with a problem statement.
  • The gaps are too tidy. When we admit something, it's a polished confession. Andreas just says it: "We never sat with an operator, and it's the biggest gap in this project."
  • The numbers are too round to be anyone's. In Okta IAM, Ads consent, Debit Card and the Okta Integration Platform most numbers are stand-ins written to sound plausible. That's the biggest risk on the site. Nothing reads more like AI to an interviewer than a number you can't explain.
  • Privacy overcorrected. It's the most honest case, but it's compressed into notes ("A list of controls in legal language, with no structure underneath."). No one is speaking. Human doesn't mean clipped.

The fix is less about word choice than about what's in the sentences: who, when, where, what went wrong, and what you'd say if someone asked "and then what?". Some of that only you know, so the rewrites below mark those spots instead of making them up.

02 · The tells

Nine patterns, with the lines they show up in

1 · The closing aphorism

That was the trade — a slower, uglier build, in exchange for never asking a customer to stop working. A checkup that nags undoes the trust it is meant to build.

Fix: end on the fact. Keep at most one line like this per case, where it's earned.

2 · "X, not Y"

The system as a contract, not a library. · I designed for confidence, not speed. · Fail at the field, not at the end.

Fix: say what you did. The contrast is the most common AI rhythm there is; it appears 6–9 times per 1,000 words in Okta IAM, Chase Pay and the Integration Platform.

3 · Always three

Renewal risk · Implementation cost · A frozen roadmap

Fix: every "What was at stake" and "What had to be solved" has exactly three points with two-word titles. Real problems come in twos and fives. Use the number there is.

4 · Em dashes

…support volume on the four highest-traffic admin flows, and — the one that reached the executive staff — deals where…

Fix: about 10 per 1,000 words in Okta IAM, the Integration Platform, Chase Pay and the archive. Use a full stop or a comma.

5 · "Genuinely", "actually"

That is genuinely good news and genuinely bad news. What the rebuilt surfaces actually do.

Fix: cut. If it's true it doesn't need the push.

6 · The polished confession

…and that was my sequencing, not their capacity.

Fix: reflections should sound like you talking after the project, not a performance review. "I'd sit with an operator first" beats a balanced sentence about ownership.

7 · Numbers no one measured

Admin experience was surfacing in 1 of every 4 enterprise renewal conversations. 64 shared components, consolidated from 212 one-off variants.

Fix: swap for your real figure, or say what you saw instead ("support kept forwarding the same three tickets"). A real small number beats a plausible big one.

8 · Abstract nouns

seam · surface · model · registers · legible · through-line

Fix: name the thing. "The screen where one squad's work met another's" instead of "the seams".

9 · One heading shape

The first structure tested well and cost a tap. I kept the groups and dropped the extra layer.

Fix: nearly every heading is two short sentences, setup then resolution. Andreas mixes: a claim, a number, a plain label ("Light or dark"), a question.

Title and opening

Rebuilding IAM for enterprise admins

Okta IAM and developer tools: rebuilding the enterprise admin console underneath live tenants — entitlements, role management, OIN publishing and developer onboarding, and the one model four squads designed to.

Okta's admin console, rebuilt in place over 15 months, with no migration day

Okta's admin console is where an IT team adds people, gives them apps, and takes access away. It was designed for companies with a few hundred employees, and Okta was now selling it to companies with tens of thousands. Six designers had fifteen months to rebuild it while every one of those companies kept using it.

How might we rebuild the screen an admin opens when something has gone wrong, without asking them to relearn it on our schedule?

Why: a scene and a constraint before any claim. The bet as one question, as Andreas does.
The problem

Okta was closing enterprise deals on a console built for smaller companies.

The administrative surfaces had been designed for an admin managing a few hundred users. The accounts Okta was now winning had tens of thousands, nested org structures, and compliance teams auditing every permission change. The same screens were being asked to do a job an order of magnitude larger.

It showed in the places that cost money: onboarding time in enterprise implementations, support volume on the four highest-traffic admin flows, and — the one that reached the executive staff — deals where a competitor demoed better against the same requirements.

What was at stake: Renewal risk · Implementation cost · A frozen roadmap

An admin at a big company rarely looks at one person. The console only knew how to show one.

At a few hundred users you can open each person's page. At forty thousand you're asking "everyone in Finance who still has Salesforce" and changing all of them at once. The console made you do that one page at a time.

No new console, no cutover date

Engineering wanted the clean version: a new console behind a flag, a migration window, a switch-over. It's easier to build and much easier to demo.

I pushed back. Nobody opens an identity console for fun. They open it because someone left the company or something leaked, and that's the worst day to find every button has moved. So we rebuilt one area at a time, on the live product.

Privacy settings people can read, and 60% fewer calls about them

PayPal's privacy page was a list of controls in legal language, and three out of four visits never got past it. Ads consent

AI-ness 6 → aim 3

How it should sound

Straight about the pressure. Growth wanted a pre-ticked box, legal wanted consent that holds up, and you put a fair ask on the table. Say it like you'd say it across a desk. Every impact number here is a stand-in, so the voice can't lean on them yet.

Andreas version
Title and opening

Privacy-first ad personalization

PayPal interest-based ads: moving ad data sharing from opted in by default to asking first, plus the Auto Privacy Advisor, a proposed checkup that brings privacy settings to people.

PayPal stopped sharing ad data until people said yes, and 41% did

PayPal shared some customer data with ad partners like Facebook and Google, and everyone was opted in unless they found the setting. About 1% ever did. In 2023 compliance flipped the default: no sharing until a customer agrees. I designed how PayPal asks.

How might we ask for consent people can say yes to and mean it, when the easiest yes is a pre-ticked box?

Why: the outcome in the title, as Andreas and your Privacy case do. "Privacy-first ad personalization" is a category label. Use it only once the 41% is real; otherwise "PayPal stopped sharing ad data until people said yes".
The problem

Everyone was opted in to ad data sharing, and almost no one knew it.

(Metrics: ~1% opted out · ~99% would leave ad audiences.)

What had to be solved: Consent people mean · A setting people can find · Honest about what changes

Ninety-nine in a hundred people were about to drop out of ad audiences

Once the default flipped, everyone who hadn't said yes was out. That was nearly everyone. Marketing felt that number immediately.

Constraints

  • Compliance set the default. Legal read every word of the ask.
  • Turning sharing off doesn't stop PayPal ads. The setting had to say so.
  • The ads settings would grow, so the structure had to take more options later.
Why: the stake told from the other side of the table, which is what makes the hard call a hard call. The three "what had to be solved" become constraints, Andreas's format.
Research

Past research first, then two days of rapid research on a prototype.

Start from what PayPal already knew. … A new structure for ads settings. … Test, then revise the consent flow. … Legal and CXR review. …

Two days, eight people

Before drawing anything we went back through PayPal's earlier privacy research and looked at how six other apps ask. Then on 19 and 20 April 2023 the researcher and I put a prototype in front of eight customers. It's a small study, and I'd call it that. The consent flow changed after it, and a third version followed. one thing someone said or did in those sessions

Why: dates and a headcount instead of four process bullets, and the size of the study named honestly ("I won't call the reviews one").
The hard call

The ask had to be worth saying yes to, without pushing anyone into it.

The easiest option was a pre-ticked box inside a vague "personalise your experience" screen… Legal backed it, because a nudged yes doesn't hold up as consent.

Part of the answer is in the setting's own words… Neither side oversells.

No pre-ticked box

Growth marketing wanted the ask inside a "personalise your experience" screen with the box already ticked. It would have kept ad audiences close to where they were, and I understood why they wanted it.

I proposed the opposite: nothing ticked, Allow and Not now the same size, and one plain sentence on what sharing means. Legal backed it. A yes someone was nudged into doesn't hold up as consent anyway.

In the sessions, 7 of 8 said they'd trust a PayPal that asked first, and the ones who chose Allow could tell us what they'd agreed to.

Why: names who wanted what, gives the other side its reason, and drops "Neither side oversells."
The checkup (Auto Privacy Advisor)

A privacy checkup that comes to people, instead of waiting to be found.

Settings only help people who go looking for them, and about 1% did. The Auto Privacy Advisor (APA) is a proposal that turns this around…

Not too often. … Not intrusive. A checkup that nags undoes the trust it is meant to build. … Tailored. …

If 1% find the setting, bring the setting to them

So I proposed a checkup. Once a year a card on Data and privacy offers a few short steps: one setting each, what it means in a line, two equal answers and Not now. It borrows from Google's Privacy Checkup, and I'd rather say so than pretend otherwise.

The risk is obvious: show it too often and it's nagging. How often is the first thing it needs testing for.

Why: the reference named plainly, and one risk said straight instead of three bulleted ones with a maxim in the middle.
