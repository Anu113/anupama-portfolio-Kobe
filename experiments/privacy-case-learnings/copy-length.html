Three ways to cut

Same story, fewer words

Option A

Trim

—words

Every block stays. Each sentence is cut to its point; rows become short phrases.

  • Least change to the page
  • Still reads as a full write-up
Recommended Option B

Captions

—words

Shorter than Gauravi's. Prose becomes captions under the visuals; repeats go; the law map and the five-question pattern (3 and 4 above) replace two blocks of words.

  • A recruiter gets it from heads and pictures
  • Needs 3, 4 and 5 built
Option C

Skim, then fold

—visible

Each section shows its head, its visual and one line. The rest, close to Option A, sits behind "More" in that section.

  • Shortest first read; the detail is one press away
  • A new fold on case pages, which you turned down once for the About bio

01 · The situation

Now

75% of settings traffic stopped on the first page, and every new rule had nowhere to go.

A list of controls in legal language, with no structure underneath. New features and new GDPR and CCPA rules had nowhere to go. Customer service heard the same privacy questions again and again.

Before / after compare

Is everything disclosed, and is what must be kept, kept?

  • Say what's collected, why, and whether it's shared
  • Honour requests to see or delete data

02 · My scope

Now

I owned the design from the first card sort to delivery, across every team with a setting on these screens.

Contextual privacy map
What I owned
The design direction, end to end: the information architecture from the card sorts, the five questions every setting answers, and the call to drop the first version's extra layer (below). I designed the Data and privacy page myself and set the direction for every flow in this case.
Research
I ran four closed card sorts and the unmoderated prototype tests, and design tests every two weeks with product managers and engineers asking the questions. I built prototypes for the researchers and helped take notes and synthesise.
How the designers worked
I set up one file system, a log of changes and a sprint planning site, so five designers could work on one set of screens. I mentored two of them on mobile patterns.
Who I worked with
A content strategist on every string, a lead UX researcher, three product managers, two data and privacy officers, more than 15 engineers, customer service, and the product teams whose features needed a setting here.
The constraint
GDPR and CCPA decide what has to be disclosed and what PayPal has to keep. Deleting your data can't remove what regulation requires PayPal to retain, and the delete screen says so.

One call: what the delete screen says

The pull
The privacy officers wanted the full retention terms on the delete screen. Product wanted "Delete and close" to stay the one way to delete.
My call
Put "Clean up your data" first, so people could delete part and keep the account, and say what PayPal must keep in one plain line, with the legal detail a link away.
What it cost
The full terms moved off the screen, and closing the account went second.
What held
Both paths shipped, and the line on what PayPal keeps is on the screen today.

03 · The hard call

Now

The first structure tested well and cost a tap. I kept the groups and dropped the extra layer.

The first version came straight from the card sorts. Four tiles: Profile privacy, Feature preferences, Notifications, and Ads and data. Each tile opened a sheet of its settings. Each setting answered five questions: what we collect, where it comes from, why, whether it's shared, and what you control.

First version phones

In rapid research the groups worked. The sheets didn't, and some labels failed too. So I kept the groups and dropped the sheets: every setting now sits on one page under three headings, Profile privacy, Permissions and shared data, and Your data rights, each with a line saying what it does.

What rapid research found, and what changed

The sheets cost one more tap before any setting, on every task.→Sheets dropped: one page, three headings.
People couldn't tell deals from ads.→Interest-based marketing became "Deals & ads" under Permissions and shared data, with "Learn how it works".
To change marketing email, people looked under Notifications, not Data and Privacy.→Emails and notifications got their own link at the bottom, where people looked.
People read "Correct your data" as something other than updating their details.→"Correct your data" became "Update your data".
2 → 1 taps · 4 → 3 groups

The card sorts settled where every setting goes, not what it's called. Two of the four findings were words: deals against ads, and "Correct your data". We tested the labels after the structure.

06 · What the law keeps

Now

Get a copy of your data, or delete part of it and keep the account, and see plainly what PayPal can't delete.

Storyboard

The storyboard the data request flow was built around: someone worried about her privacy, who wants to see what PayPal holds and pick what to ask for.

Every request passes through six steps

Intake
The request comes in through the intake portal.
Verification
PayPal confirms it's really the customer asking.
Validation
The requester is a PayPal customer, or a guest account holder.
Eligibility
The request meets the criteria.
Fulfilment
The data is gathered, or erased.
Communication
The customer hears the outcome.

The customer gets status updates along the way, and a confirmation when it's done.

Deletion had to work in parts. People asked to erase specific kinds of data, like face scan, marketing, package tracking and third-party profile data, and keep using their account. Regulation decides what PayPal must keep, so the flow says what will be deleted and what can't be.

Erasing only part
Delete marketing data, keep transaction history and the account.
A request left halfway
Someone starts and doesn't finish, and gets a reminder to complete it.
A second request
A new request arrives while the first is still processing.
What the law keeps
Data PayPal must retain is named, not silently left behind.

08 · What I'd do differently

Now

The groups held up early. The words took longer. I'd test them together, and rougher.

Test the words with the groups
The card sorts settled where every setting goes. Rapid research then found the words failing: people couldn't tell deals from ads, and read "Correct your data" as something else. I'd test labels with the content strategist from the first round, not after the structure.
Go rougher before high fidelity
Three options became high-fidelity prototypes before the unmoderated test, and two missed the 70% bar. Clickable wireframes would have found that sooner, for less.
